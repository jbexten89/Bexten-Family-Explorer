Two families, one wedding

On the evening of December 4, 1954, at Central Avenue Methodist Church in Toledo, a 21-year-old farmer from Ottawa Lake, Michigan, married a telephone operator who was turning 20 that very day. Their names were Anthony Joseph "Tony" Bexten and Betty A. “Althea” Bonnell, who went by her middle name, and their marriage joined two families that could hardly have come from more different places.

Tony's people were newcomers. His grandfather had crossed the Atlantic as a boy of about twelve in 1880, alone, and by family tradition as a stowaway. The rest of Tony's family were German Catholic farmers on the Michigan line and a big Catholic household in Toledo, headed by a city fireman.

Althea's people had been in America for three hundred years. Her family tree runs back through the hills of central West Virginia to Revolutionary War soldiers, frontier settlers who lived through Shawnee raids, French Protestant refugees, and finally to a Pilgrim family of Plymouth Colony.

This booklet tells both stories, oldest first. It was put together in November 2026 from census records, death certificates, marriage licenses, church registers, newspaper obituaries and published family histories. Where something is family tradition or not yet proven, the text says so.

1630: Pilgrims and Puritans

The oldest documented line in the family begins with Thomas Blossom, one of the English Separatists who fled to Leiden, Holland, and later joined the Pilgrims of Plymouth Colony. His daughter Elizabeth Blossom was born in Leiden.

Thomas was born about 1580, probably at Great Shelford, near Cambridge, and married Anne Elsdon at St. Clement's Church in Cambridge in 1605. By 1609 they had left England for Leiden to worship freely with the Separatist congregation, and in 1610 he sent Anne back to sell the Cambridge houses she had inherited. Family accounts say Thomas set out for America with the first Pilgrims in 1620, on the Speedwell, the leaky ship that had to turn back. He finally reached Plymouth in 1629 on a later ship also named Mayflower. There he was remembered as "a holy man and experienced saint." He died in the fever epidemic of 1633.

Edward Fitz Randolph's family came from Sutton-in-Ashfield, a Nottinghamshire village where the parish register records his baptism on July 5, 1607, as "Edward Fitzrandall son of Edward." His parents were Edward Fitz Randolph and Frances Howes.

In 1630, Edward Fitz Randolph, baptized in 1607 at Sutton-in-Ashfield in Nottinghamshire, England, sailed to Massachusetts with the Winthrop Fleet, the great Puritan migration that founded Boston. He married Elizabeth Blossom in 1637. They lived at Scituate and Barnstable on Cape Cod, then moved to Piscataway, New Jersey, where Edward died around 1685. They are 8th great-grandparents of Tony and Althea's children, and Thomas Blossom is their 9th great-grandfather.

Their son Thomas Fitz Randolph was born at Barnstable in 1659 and died at Piscataway in 1745. Two of Thomas's sons, Jonathan and David, each had a child who would marry the other: Jonathan's son Samuel (born 1738) married David's daughter Margaret (born 1739) in 1761. They were first cousins, so Tony and Althea's children descend from Thomas Fitz Randolph twice.

Samuel and Margaret, Seventh Day Baptists, eventually left New Jersey for the western Virginia mountains, as the next chapters tell.

1733–1795: The Virginia frontier

The Bonnets and the Waggoners arrive

On August 27, 1733, the ship Elizabeth docked at Philadelphia carrying Jacques (John) Bonnet and his wife Marie Desreux, French Protestants who had been living in Baden, Germany. Two of their little daughters had died on the voyage. Their son Samuel Bonnett grew up to farm on the South Branch of the Potomac and later in Harrison County, Virginia, where he was killed by a falling tree in 1789.

Fifteen years after the Bonnets, on October 25, 1748, Wilhelm Waggoner, 42, arrived at Philadelphia on the Patience and Margaret from Rotterdam. He had married at Ober-Seemen in Hesse. His son John Waggoner was born in 1751 at White Marsh, Pennsylvania. Wilhelm moved the family to the South Branch frontier, and there, around 1755, he was killed and scalped in an Indian raid on his farm.

John Waggoner married Samuel Bonnett's daughter Margaret, and after the Revolution they settled on Jesse's Run, a branch of Hacker's Creek in what is now Lewis County, West Virginia.

The raid of 1792

In May 1792 a Shawnee raiding party struck the Waggoner place. John Waggoner later swore in his pension declaration that Tecumseh led it. Margaret was killed, along with several of her children. Three children were carried off: Elizabeth, 12, her sister Mary, and their little brother Peter, about 6.

Elizabeth was taken west to the Shawnee towns. Family accounts say she was sold to a harsh mistress, and that Tecumseh himself once stepped in when she was being beaten. In the fall of 1793 she escaped with another captive and made her way to Detroit, where she stayed until the 1795 peace. She was then brought back to her father. Her sister Mary came home after the Treaty of Greenville. Her brother Peter did not come back until 1812; by then he had a Shawnee wife and children of his own.

The war behind the raid. The Waggoner raid came in the worst years of what historians call the Northwest Indian War (1785–1795), a fight between the young United States and a confederacy of Shawnee, Miami, Delaware and other nations over the Ohio country. The tribes crushed two American armies in 1790 and 1791, and settlers in western Virginia lived in fear of renewed raids. The war ended on August 20, 1794, when Gen. Anthony Wayne defeated the confederacy at the Battle of Fallen Timbers, fought near present-day Toledo, Ohio, close to where Elizabeth's descendants would one day live. The Treaty of Greenville followed on August 3, 1795. Captives were returned, the Ohio tribes gave up most of present-day Ohio, and for the first time western Virginia families were safe from raids. Tecumseh refused to sign it.

In 1798 Elizabeth married John Dexter Hardman, a millwright who became a Methodist preacher. They had 13 children. Her own account of the captivity was written down in the family, and her son Perry obtained a typed copy in 1920. Elizabeth Waggoner Hardman died in 1854. She is a 4th great-grandmother of Tony and Althea's children.

Soldiers in the family

Two 5th great-grandfathers of Tony and Althea's children are recognized patriots of the American Revolution. Either one would qualify a descendant to join the Sons or Daughters of the American Revolution.

A third has now turned up in the pension files: John Bonnell (1761–1823), Samuel Fitz Randolph's son-in-law. In 1818, at 57, he told the Harrison County court that he had enlisted about 1782 in Captain Machin's company of Col. John Lamb's New York artillery regiment, part of the Continental Army, and served to the end of the war, when he was discharged at West Point. His 1820 schedule lists his son Charles, then 12, the next generation in the line.

John Waggoner (1751–1841), Elizabeth's father, served Virginia as a private. In 1778 he was a ranger and spy on the frontier. He served in Capt. John Harness's company, was at the Siege of Yorktown in 1781, and later scouted for raiders as an "Indian spy" at Vest's Fort. He drew a federal pension (S7824) in his old age. (SAR patriot #P-338764.)

Samuel Fitz Randolph (1738–1825) served New Jersey as an ensign in the 2nd Regiment of the Sussex County militia and as a captain in the battalion north of the Raritan River in 1775. After the war he and Margaret moved to western Virginia, where they were among the founders of New Salem, now Salem, West Virginia. Both are buried in the Seventh Day Baptist cemetery there. (SAR patriot #P-158994, DAR #A040292.)

His muster papers survive too. At West Point on February 26, 1783, Captain G. Fleming of the New York artillery certified that he had that day mustered "John Bonnell a recruit in said Regiment, inlisted by Capt. Thomas Machin for during the present War." John married Samuel Fitz Randolph's daughter Rulanah.

In the Civil War, Rulanah's grandson Floyd Neely Bonnell (1839–1929) enlisted on October 28, 1861, as a private in Company B, 6th West Virginia Infantry, on the Union side, and served until 1864. He lived to be 90 and is buried in Machpelah Cemetery in Weston.

The 6th West Virginia Infantry. Floyd's regiment was raised between August and December 1861 to protect the Baltimore & Ohio Railroad, the Union's vital link between Washington and the West. It was one of the largest infantry regiments the Union recruited, but it never fought as a single unit. Its companies were spread out guarding bridges, tunnels and depots along the line, and they skirmished again and again with Confederate raiders and bushwhackers, including twice at Weston in the late summer of 1862. Disease was far deadlier than the enemy: the regiment lost 8 men killed in battle and 169 to illness. It was mustered out in June 1865.

In the twentieth century, the Bexten children's great-grandfather Herbert H. Smith of Toledo served in World War I.

1799–1900: Mountain families

The Beamers of Monroe County

Around 1799, Philip Beamer (1763–1838) and his wife Elizabeth loaded a big four-horse wagon at Reading, Pennsylvania, and set out for Tennessee. They never got there. Finding no place to their liking, they settled instead on Plank Cabin Draft near Hillsdale in Monroe County, Virginia, and lived for two years in a hunter's cabin with an acre of clearing. The family was German; the name was probably Böhmer in Pennsylvania, though the link to the 1700s Böhmer immigrants is not yet proven.

Their son Philip Jr. married Elspeth Ballantyne, and they named a son Andrew Ballentine Beamer (1821–1904) after her family. Andrew married Rebecca Jemima Erwin in Greenbrier County in 1845, and their son Calvin Byrd Beamer (1861–1924) carried his grandmother Catharine Byrd's surname as a middle name. Calvin married Cora Frances Forinash in Braxton County in May 1891. He farmed and died at Weston in 1924.

Forinash and Hardman

Cora came from two old Lewis County families. Her great-great-grandfather Charles Forinash claimed 400 acres on the Buckhannon River in 1772, when the area was still the far edge of Virginia. His son Jacob Forinash (1796–1837 or 1838) shows up on both sides of Cora's tree: her father William Dexter Forinash was Jacob's grandson through his first wife, and her mother Cynthia Jane Hardman was Jacob's granddaughter through his second wife. Cynthia was also a granddaughter of Elizabeth Waggoner, the captive of 1792.

Bonnell and May

On the other side, Rulanah Fitz Randolph and John Bonnell's son Charles Bonnell married Jane Martin in Harrison County in 1829 and raised twelve children. One was Floyd Neely Bonnell, the Union soldier. In 1863 Floyd married a young widow, Elizabeth Jane Pitman, born Goldsborough in New Market, Virginia, whose first husband had died that January. She had lost a great deal young. Her father, James Goldsberry, died in Barbour County by 1846, when she was about thirteen, and his small estate (a few cows, beds and quilts, a churn and a tea set) went to a neighbor to settle. By 1850 her younger sisters Rebecca and Christena were living with other families, and Elizabeth, at seventeen, was already married to Isaac Pitman with a baby son. Her own 1863 marriage record names her parents as James and Margaret, and so do her sisters'. Their son John William Jasper Bonnell (1866–1947) married Amanda Ann May in Lewis County in 1896.

Amanda's parents, John May and Sarah Johnson, were both born in the fall of 1852, he in Nicholas County and she in Roane County. They married in Braxton County in 1872, lived their last years in Clarksburg, and are buried in Weston.

1860s: The Grafs come to Whiteford Township

Dad's side of the family begins in America with Joseph Graf, born on March 16, 1834, in Baden, in southwestern Germany, the son of George Graf, and Brigitta Reger, born on March 28, 1832, in neighboring Württemberg, the daughter of John Reger. Brigitta had been married before, to a man named Jaeger, and had two daughters, Maria and Teresia, born between about 1858 and 1861. The 1870 census says both girls were born in Michigan, so Brigitta may already have been living there by the late 1850s.

Joseph and Brigitta married about 1862, and their daughter Rosa was born on May 6, 1864. Most later records say she was born in New York, but the 1870 census says Michigan, so her birthplace is not certain. By 1870 the family was farming in Whiteford Township in Monroe County, Michigan, just across the state line from Toledo. Joseph farmed 80 acres there, and the family belonged to St. Joseph Catholic Church in Sylvania, Ohio. The parish's Latin family register calls him Badensis, "the man from Baden." Rosa's half-sisters both married into the Hecker family nearby.

On the morning of December 11, 1905, Joseph, 71, was driving his horse and buggy from the farm into Sylvania. At the Lake Shore railroad crossing on Indiana Street he failed to see a passenger train coming, and he and the horse were killed instantly. That evening's Toledo Blade ran the story under the headline "Joseph Graff Met His Death," calling him a well-to-do farmer and a member of the Catholic church at Sylvania; the buggy, it said, was "smashed to kindling-wood." He was buried at Sylvania two days later.

Brigitta lived another eight years. She died at Whiteford on January 25, 1914, at 81, and was buried at St. Joseph's in Sylvania. The person who reported her death was Mrs. William Berry of Ottawa Lake, born Rosa Hecker, most likely Brigitta's granddaughter through one of the Jaeger girls. Exactly where in Baden and Württemberg Joseph and Brigitta came from, and when they crossed the ocean, are still unknown.

1880: The stowaway

Anthony Joseph Bexten was born on June 16, 1867, in Westphalia, a Catholic farming region of northwestern Germany. His father was Herman Bexten. He had at least three sisters.

His hometown may finally have turned up. The baptism books of St. Lambertus, the Catholic church in the small town of Stromberg near Oelde, record Caspar Anton Bexte, born July 16, 1868, son of Hermann Bexte and Catharina Rüdingloh, who had married in 1860. The 1900 census gives Anthony's birth as July 1868, and the couple's other children included four daughters, Elisabeth, Johanna, Anna Antonia and Anna Sophia, and twin sons, Bernard and August. Then Anthony's own 1899 marriage record turned up. It names his parents as “Hamlin Baxter” and “Chaterine Redingloch,” a county clerk's spelling of Hermann Bexte and Catharina Rüdingloh. With both parents' names matching, the Stromberg baptism is almost certainly his; the original church book, with any notes beside the entry, would make it certain. The same church books suggest why a boy of twelve might set out alone: by 1870 his mother had remarried, to Carl Sander, and between 1870 and 1875 she had four more children with him at Stromberg. Hermann, it seems, had died while Anthony was still a baby.

In 1880, when he was about twelve, Anthony came to America by himself. Tony Bexten, his grandson, used to tell how his grandfather "stole away on a ship and was found halfway across the ocean." The records fit the story. No passenger list has been found for him, no parent or brother ever appears in an American record, and when he died in 1950 his three sisters were still living in Germany. A boy who left that young, that alone, may never have spoken much about the family he left behind: when his son Nelson filled out his death certificate, he could name Anthony's father but wrote "Don't know" for his mother.

Crossing in 1880. Anthony left Germany at the start of the largest wave of German emigration to America, which peaked in the early 1880s. Most German emigrants then sailed by steamship from Bremen or Hamburg, a crossing of roughly two weeks, packed by the hundreds into steerage below decks. Arrivals at New York landed at Castle Garden, the immigrant depot at the tip of Manhattan; Ellis Island did not open until 1892. A stowaway's lot was harder. Boys who hid in the coal bunkers or among the crew's bedding were usually found within a few days and put to work, often helping the stokers shovel coal, to pay for their passage. Stowaways found before the ship left coastal waters were often put ashore with the harbor pilot. Once a ship was far out at sea there was no turning back, and on arrival stowaways were handed over to the immigration authorities. That fits the family story: Anthony was "found halfway across the ocean."

Anthony found his way to Whiteford Township, the farm country around Ottawa Lake, Michigan, and was a naturalized citizen by 1900. On April 11, 1899, he married Rosa Graf, the farmer's daughter from the next place over. In the 1900 census the newlyweds appear on the same page as Rosa's parents.

Anthony and Rosa farmed on Yankee Road at Ottawa Lake for the rest of their lives and raised four children: Joseph Herman (1900–1989), Nelson (1903–1987), Mary Theresia (1905–1992, later Mrs. Arnold C. Miller) and Lawrence Arnold (1909–1999). Rosa was active at St. Joseph's in Sylvania, and the parish Altar Society met at her house.

Rosa died at home on August 20, 1945, after a short illness, at 81. The Toledo Blade noted that she "had lived in the same vicinity all her life." The Altar Society women said the rosary at the house. Anthony died at home on Sunday, January 8, 1950, at 82, a retired farmer who had spent most of his life in the Ottawa Lake area. He left four children, 12 grandchildren, 2 great-grandchildren, and three sisters an ocean away. Both are buried in St. Joseph's Cemetery, Sylvania.

The fireman's daughter

On Tony's mother's side, the family were city people. Herbert Henry Smith was born in Toledo on October 5, 1889, and lived there his whole life. He served in World War I, then joined the Toledo Fire Department, where he worked for 17 years, mostly at the No. 2 and No. 3 engine houses, until 1937.

Herbert was the son of Frank J. Smith and Caroline "Carrie" Langenderfer. Frank was born on February 19, 1865, in Monroe, Michigan, to German parents, Wendell Smith (originally Schmidt) and Barbara Ankenbrandt. Frank and Carrie married in 1888 and raised Frank, Herbert, Olive, Earl and Mildred in Toledo. Carrie was born on September 13, 1868, at Raab, Ohio, the daughter of Wendel and Mary (Anna Maria) Langenderfer, who came from the same hamlet, Hofeld-Mauschbach near Sankt Wendel in the Prussian Rhineland, married there in 1849, and arrived at New York together in September 1850, Wendel a 22-year-old watchmaker. Frank belonged to the Woodworkers' union and the Woodmen of the World. He died at St. Vincent's Hospital on March 18, 1931, after ten years of illness, and was buried from St. Mary's Church. Carrie stayed on in their house on Moore Street until her own death on September 12, 1945, and is buried in Mount Carmel Cemetery.

Herbert and his wife Mary A. Miller, called Mamie, born in Ohio about 1891, raised nine children in Toledo: Gertrude ("Aunt DoDo"), Caroline, Lucille, Kathryn, Mildred ("Aunt PeeWee"), Sylvester, Roland, Frederick ("Fritz") and Al ("Whitey"), who appears in the 1950 census as Aloisius H. Smith. In the 1940 census the family was living in Ward 3 with six of the children still at home. They were Catholics at St. Mary's Church.

Herbert spent his last three years ill at the Ohio Soldiers and Sailors Home in Sandusky, and died there on June 3, 1949, at 59. He is buried in Mt. Carmel Cemetery. His obituary in the Toledo Blade lists "Mrs. Caroline Bexten, Ottawa Lake, Mich." first among his daughters. He was survived by a brother, Earl, of Maumee, and two sisters, Mrs. Leonard Ackermann of Toledo and Mrs. Carl Rise of St. Petersburg, Florida. Herbert and Mamie had married at Monroe, Michigan, on February 3, 1908, a month before Sylvester was born; the record names Mamie's parents as Fred Miller and Margaret Ackerman. In 1900 the Millers owned a house at 938 Frederick Street in Toledo, where Fred worked as a carriage woodworker; Mamie, the eldest of five, lived there with her parents and her grandfather Louis Ackerman, who had come from Alsace, then part of France, about 1850. Louis and his wife Augusta Datzel, who was from Bavaria, had raised six children in Toledo's 4th Ward; Augusta died in 1898 and Louis in 1904, and Fred (1923) and Margaret (1952) were buried near him at Calvary Cemetery.

Nelson and Caroline

Nelson A. Bexten, Anthony and Rosa's second son, was born in Monroe County, Michigan, on June 3, 1903, and grew up on the farm at Ottawa Lake. On August 8, 1928, in Lucas County, Ohio, he married Caroline M. Smith, the fireman's daughter from Toledo, who was 18.

They started out near Ottawa Lake, where they were living in 1934, moved into Toledo by 1942, and in 1958 settled on a farm in Madison Township, near Pioneer, Ohio, where they belonged to Sacred Heart Catholic Church in Montpelier. They had five children: Tony (1933–2011), Joanne Lucille (1934–2020, later Johnson), Carl, called Carly, who died as a baby in 1936, a son born about 1939 and Patricia Ann (1942–2024, later Delcamp).

Nelson and Caroline died within months of each other in 1987. Caroline died on May 17. Nelson, by then retired from farming and living on North State Street in Pioneer, died early on Tuesday, November 24, at Community Hospitals of Williams County in Bryan after a long illness, at 84. His funeral was at Sacred Heart in Montpelier, and he is buried in St. Joseph's Cemetery, Sylvania, with his parents. His obituary counted 13 grandchildren and 10 great-grandchildren.

Weston to Toledo

Onal Bonnell, son of John W. J. Bonnell and Amanda May, was born in Lewis County on January 6, 1906. He married Genevieve Virginia Beamer, Calvin and Cora's daughter, born May 23, 1906, at Newville in Braxton County. They settled in Weston, where Onal worked as a glassworker, and had four children: Robert Eugene (1927–2007), Wallace (1928–1983), Charles Eddie, who died in 1933 at about two, and Betty A. “Althea”, born December 4, 1934, who always went by her middle name.

Both came from big families. Onal was the fifth of nine children born to John and Amanda Bonnell, after Dorsey, Myrtle, Dorothy Iva and Mamie, and before Ruth, Carl and the twins John Franklin and Joseph Cure; he also had three older half-siblings, Earnest, Jesse and Retta. Genevieve was the youngest of Calvin and Cora's children, after Blanche, Raymond, Bertha and Vada, and had two older half-sisters, Lora and Bessie, from Calvin's first marriage to Elizabeth Gillespie.

Glass town. When Onal went to work, Weston was one of the glassmaking centers of West Virginia. More than twenty glass companies opened there after 1903, making window glass, lamp chimneys, milk bottles and fine tableware. The best known was founded by Louie Wohinc, an Austrian immigrant who had started working in a glass factory at age nine. In the 1920s and 1930s his Weston factories turned out about 144,000 pieces of glass a day, all by hand. By the 1930s West Virginia had about 13,000 glassworkers, most of them union members. Which Weston factory Onal worked in is not yet known.

On February 15, 1940, Onal died at Weston General Hospital. He was 34. A few weeks later the census taker found Genevieve, 33, a widow, heading the household in Weston with Robert, 13, Wallace, 11, and Althea, 5. He is buried in Machpelah Cemetery in Weston, near his Civil War grandfather.

Genevieve eventually took the family north to the Toledo area. By 1951 Althea was in Williams County, Ohio, and by 1954 she and her mother were living on Ambia Street in Toledo, where Althea worked as a telephone operator. Genevieve was still "Mrs. Genevieve Bonnell" when her daughter married, but on June 15, 1957, at Angola, Indiana, she married John Rosentreter. She worked for 25 years as a caterer for the Gladieux Corporation, retiring in 1970. She outlived John and three of her four children, and died at home on Navarre Avenue in Oregon, Ohio, on October 7, 1990, at 84. She is buried in Ottawa Hills Memorial Park.

Tony and Althea

Anthony Joseph "Tony" Bexten, Nelson and Caroline's eldest, was born on March 28, 1933, and was farming at Ottawa Lake when he met Betty A. “Althea” Bonnell. Their marriage license appeared in the Toledo Blade in late 1954: "Anthony J. Bexten, 21, farmer, Ottawa Lake, Mich., and Betty A. Bonnell, 19, telephone operator, 918 Ambia St."

They were married on the evening of December 4, 1954, Althea's 20th birthday, at Central Avenue Methodist Church in Toledo. The Blade's wedding notice said the bride wore a gown of Chantilly lace and nylon tulle over satin.

Tony and Althea raised six children. Althea died in Toledo on January 28, 1983, at only 48. Later that year, on September 10, 1983, Tony married Eleanor M. Eidenier. Tony died on March 29, 2011, the day after his 78th birthday, and is buried, like Althea, in Floral Grove Cemetery in Pioneer, Ohio. Eleanor died in Pioneer on May 17, 2020, at 84.

It was Tony who kept his grandfather's story alive: the boy from Westphalia who stowed away on a ship and was found halfway across the ocean.

Still to discover

Some questions are still open. If you have letters, a family Bible, photographs, or a story you heard growing up, they may hold the answers.

  • Where in Westphalia was Anthony Bexten born? The baptism books of Liesborn and Ennigerloh, two Catholic parishes with old Bexten farms, have been checked for 1867 and 1868 without finding him. Almost certainly Stromberg: his 1899 marriage record names his parents as Hermann and Catharina (Rüdingloh), the same couple as the July 1868 Stromberg baptism of Caspar Anton Bexte. The original church book would confirm it.
  • Who were Anthony's sisters and mother? Their descendants may still live in Germany.
  • Where in Baden and Württemberg were Joseph Graf and Brigitta Reger from? A Monroe County probate file for Joseph's 1905 estate may help.
  • Which village in Alsace was Louis Ackerman from, and where in Bavaria did John Miller and Catharina Friedrich come from?
  • Did the Beamers descend from the 1700s Böhmer immigrants of Pennsylvania? One record suggests the family may have come from Germany later.
  • Who were John Bonnell’s parents in New Jersey? His Revolutionary service is now proven by his pension file, and it makes him a third patriot ancestor.
